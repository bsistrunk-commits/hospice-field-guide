🏛️ Medicare Hospice Eligibility

The basic Medicare test

A Medicare beneficiary generally qualifies to elect the Part A hospice benefit when all of these are true:

  • The patient is entitled to Medicare Part A.
  • The patient uses a Medicare-certified hospice.
  • The required physician(s) certify that the patient is terminally ill, meaning a prognosis of 6 months or less if the illness runs its normal course.
  • The patient or representative elects hospice in writing.
  • The patient accepts the hospice framework for the terminal illness and related conditions—comfort/palliation rather than Medicare payment for treatment intended to cure those conditions outside the hospice arrangement.
Critical concept: “Six months” is a prognosis, not a countdown clock. A patient can remain on hospice beyond six months when the patient continues to be recertified as terminally ill.

Certification: who can do what?

Initial certification

For the first 90-day period, the hospice must obtain certification of terminal illness from the hospice medical director or physician member of the hospice interdisciplinary group, and from the patient’s attending physician if the patient has one.

Under current CMS manual guidance, only an MD or DO can certify or recertify terminal illness. Nurse practitioners and physician assistants cannot perform the certification itself, although an NP can perform the required face-to-face encounter for later recertifications.

What the written certification must contain
  • A statement that life expectancy is 6 months or less if the terminal illness runs its normal course.
  • Patient-specific clinical findings supporting that prognosis.
  • Physician signature and date.
  • The benefit-period dates covered.
  • A brief individualized narrative explaining the clinical basis for the prognosis.

CMS specifically warns against generic, boilerplate certification narratives that do not synthesize the patient’s individual clinical circumstances.

Eligibility is not a checklist score

No single PPS number, FAST stage, lab value, diagnosis, hospitalization count, or symptom automatically creates hospice eligibility. The physician’s prognosis is based on the whole clinical picture and trajectory. MAC Local Coverage Determinations (LCDs) can provide disease-specific documentation guidance, but they should not be treated as a substitute for individualized clinical judgment.

📅 Benefit Periods & Recertification

Benefit-period structure

PeriodLengthWhat happens
1st90 daysInitial certification and election.
2nd90 daysRecertification for continued terminal prognosis.
3rd60 daysRecertification + required face-to-face encounter.
4th and every later period60 days eachUnlimited number if the patient remains eligible; face-to-face required before each recertification.

There is no lifetime six-month limit. Medicare permits an unlimited number of 60-day periods after the first two 90-day periods as long as the patient remains terminally ill and required recertifications are completed.

Face-to-face (FTF) essentials

  • Required before the 3rd benefit period and before every subsequent benefit period.
  • Performed by a hospice physician or hospice nurse practitioner.
  • Generally must occur within the 30 calendar days before the recertification period begins.
  • The clinical findings from the encounter must support the continued prognosis of 6 months or less.
  • Failure to meet the FTF requirement can break eligibility/payment until the requirement is satisfied and the patient is appropriately re-elected, if still eligible.
Upcoming rule note: CMS finalized telehealth-related FTF changes for implementation beginning January 1, 2027. This guide is dated September 11, 2026, so always verify the rule in effect on the date of service.

Certification timing pearls

Initial period

CMS permits the initial certification to be completed up to 15 days before the hospice election. Required oral or written certification must be obtained no later than 2 calendar days after hospice care is initiated, with the written certification in the record before the claim is submitted.

Later periods

Recertification may be completed up to 15 days before the next benefit period begins. A written certification is required for each benefit period.

✍️ Election, Revocation, Transfer & Discharge

What the election means

The hospice election is a patient choice. It identifies the hospice, establishes the effective date, acknowledges the palliative nature of hospice, and explains that Medicare payment for care related to the terminal illness and related conditions is generally routed through the designated hospice/attending arrangement.

Important election-statement elements
  • Hospice identification.
  • Effective date—no retroactive election.
  • Attending physician designation, if any.
  • Acknowledgment of the palliative rather than curative focus.
  • Explanation of Medicare services waived by election.
  • Cost-sharing information.
  • BFCC-QIO rights and contact information.
  • Patient/representative signature.
Notice of Election (NOE)

The hospice files a Notice of Election with its Medicare Administrative Contractor, generally within 5 calendar days after the election date. Late filing can make the hospice financially liable for affected days.

Revocation

Only the patient or representative revokes hospice. A hospice does not “revoke” a patient.

  • Revocation must be written and signed.
  • It must state the effective date; it cannot be made retroactive.
  • The patient gives up the remaining days in that specific election period.
  • Regular Medicare coverage for the previously waived benefits resumes.
  • There is no waiting period to elect hospice again if the patient remains eligible.

Transfer to another hospice

A patient may change the designated hospice once in each election period. A transfer is not a revocation and does not reset the benefit period. The patient files a signed statement naming the old hospice, the new hospice, and the effective date of transfer.

Live discharge

A hospice cannot simply discharge a patient because care is expensive, inconvenient, or because length of stay is long. CMS permits discharge in defined circumstances, including:

  • The patient moves outside the hospice service area or transfers.
  • The patient is determined to be no longer terminally ill.
  • Discharge for cause under extraordinary circumstances after required attempts to resolve problems and protect patient/staff safety.
Field pearl: A patient getting “better” enough to no longer meet the terminal prognosis is a legitimate live-discharge scenario. Discharge to avoid cost or cap exposure is not.

Election-statement addendum & relatedness

The addendum explains which diagnoses, items, services, and drugs the hospice considers unrelated to the terminal illness and related conditions—and therefore outside hospice coverage.

Important 2026 change: CMS finalized a rule making the addendum mandatory for all Medicare hospice elections beginning October 1, 2026. The August 2026 CMS model states it must be provided within the first 5 days of the election start date, with updates when plan-of-care changes affect those determinations.

🧰 What the Hospice Benefit Covers

Core covered services

When reasonable and necessary for palliation and management of the terminal illness and related conditions, the hospice benefit can include:

  • Hospice physician and certain practitioner services
  • Nursing care
  • Medical equipment and supplies
  • Drugs and biologicals for pain and symptom management
  • Hospice aide and homemaker services
  • Medical social services
  • Dietary counseling
  • Spiritual counseling
  • Grief and bereavement counseling for patient/family before and after death
  • Physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech-language pathology when part of the hospice plan of care
  • Short-term inpatient care for symptom management (GIP)
  • Short-term inpatient respite
  • Other reasonable and necessary services included in the individualized plan of care

Related vs unrelated care

Once hospice is elected, the hospice is expected to provide or arrange virtually all care related to the terminal illness and related conditions. Truly unrelated care can still be covered through regular Medicare, with ordinary deductibles/coinsurance applying.

Practical rule: Before a hospice patient goes to the ED, is hospitalized, takes an ambulance, starts a new medication, or sees another provider for something that may be related to the terminal diagnosis, the safest workflow is to coordinate with the hospice first. Otherwise coverage problems can result.

What Medicare hospice generally does not cover

  • Treatment intended to cure the terminal illness or related conditions outside the hospice arrangement.
  • Hospice care from a second hospice that has not been arranged by the designated hospice.
  • Hospital/ED/ambulance care related to the terminal condition when not arranged by the hospice, absent an applicable exception.
  • Routine room and board in the patient’s home, assisted living, nursing facility, or residential hospice setting.

Room and board exception: Medicare can cover the inpatient facility stay when the hospice arranges covered GIP or inpatient respite.

Beneficiary cost-sharing

  • $0 for most covered hospice services from a Medicare-approved hospice.
  • Hospice may charge up to $5 per prescription for outpatient drugs/biologicals used for pain and symptom control during RHC/CHC, subject to CMS rules.
  • For inpatient respite, the patient may owe 5% of the Medicare-approved amount, subject to the annual inpatient-deductible cap.

👥 IDG & Plan of Care

The hospice interdisciplinary group

CMS requires an interdisciplinary approach addressing physical, medical, psychosocial, emotional, and spiritual needs. The core IDG includes:

  • A physician (MD/DO) employed by or under contract with the hospice.
  • A registered nurse.
  • A social worker.
  • A pastoral or other counselor.

An RN member of the IDG coordinates care and helps ensure continuous assessment and implementation of the plan.

Individualized plan of care (POC)

The plan of care is built around the patient’s individual needs and goals and is established by the hospice IDG together with the attending physician, if any, the patient/representative, and primary caregiver.

What the POC concept means in practice
  • Symptoms and comfort goals drive interventions.
  • Visit frequency/intensity should match current needs.
  • Medication, DME, supplies, counseling, therapies, and other services are coordinated rather than fragmented.
  • The plan changes as the patient changes.
  • The hospice remains responsible for coordinating hospice-related care even when the patient lives in a facility.
Nursing facility / assisted-living pearl

Hospice does not replace the facility’s responsibilities. The hospice and facility coordinate so everyone understands who is providing which service. Routine room and board generally remains separate from the Medicare hospice benefit.

🏠 The Four Medicare Levels of Care

At-a-glance comparison

LevelMain purposeTypical settingKey Medicare concept
Routine Home Care (RHC)Baseline hospice care when no higher level is medically necessary.Private home, ALF, SNF/NF, residential setting—wherever the patient calls home.Most hospice days are RHC. Payment is a daily hospice rate, not a 24/7 bedside-staff guarantee.
Continuous Home Care (CHC)Manage an acute symptom crisis while keeping the patient at home.Patient’s home/residence, including certain facilities.Brief crisis care; at least 8 hours in a 24-hour day, predominantly nursing care under CMS rules.
Inpatient RespiteGive the usual caregiver temporary relief.Approved inpatient facility, hospital, hospice inpatient facility, or qualifying nursing facility.Occasional short-term relief; Medicare payment at respite level limited to 5 consecutive days at a time.
General Inpatient Care (GIP)Manage pain or acute/chronic symptoms that cannot feasibly be managed in another setting.Contracted hospital, hospice inpatient facility, or qualifying facility.Must be driven by symptom-management need, not simply by prognosis, caregiver convenience, or placement issues.

Common level-of-care misconceptions

“Hospice means 24-hour caregivers at home.”

No. Routine hospice is intermittent interdisciplinary care plus 24/7 availability for support. CHC is a special, time-limited crisis level—not the default hospice model.

“The patient is actively dying, therefore it’s GIP.”

Not automatically. GIP hinges on symptoms or care needs that cannot feasibly be managed elsewhere at that time. Imminence of death alone is not the criterion.

“The family is exhausted, so use GIP.”

Caregiver exhaustion points more naturally toward respite if respite criteria are met. GIP requires symptom-management need.

“CHC means a sitter because the family can’t be there.”

No. CHC is for a period of crisis requiring predominantly nursing care to achieve palliation or symptom management.

🏥 GIP Deep Dive

The core Medicare concept

GIP is short-term inpatient hospice care for pain control or acute/chronic symptom management that cannot feasibly be managed in another setting.

Not enough by itself: “Patient is dying,” “family wants patient to stay in the hospital,” “no discharge destination,” or “caregiver unavailable.” Those facts may matter to disposition planning, but they do not independently establish GIP.

Examples that can raise the GIP question

  • Severe pain requiring rapid parenteral titration or frequent skilled reassessment.
  • Uncontrolled dyspnea requiring intensive management.
  • Severe agitation/delirium/restlessness that remains uncontrolled despite reasonable lower-level interventions.
  • Intractable nausea/vomiting or other symptoms requiring inpatient stabilization.
  • Medication adjustment, observation, or stabilizing treatment that cannot feasibly occur elsewhere.
  • Complex wounds/bleeding or other acute symptom situations requiring inpatient-level intervention.
  • A temporary breakdown in home support when that breakdown makes it infeasible to safely furnish the needed skilled symptom-management care at home.

Examples are recognition cues—not automatic coverage rules. The hospice must document the patient-specific symptom-management need and why another setting is not feasible.

Hospital GIP workflow

  1. Identify a possible hospice-appropriate patient and possible GIP-level need.
  2. Clarify goals: Does patient/family understand hospice and want comfort-focused care?
  3. Clinical assessment: Hospice clinician evaluates hospice eligibility and current level-of-care need.
  4. Certification/election: Complete required physician certification and beneficiary election.
  5. Coordinate with hospital: Clarify orders, attending/hospice roles, medication plan, documentation, contract/arrangement, and communication pathway.
  6. Admit at GIP if supported.
  7. Actively manage symptoms with ongoing skilled assessment.
  8. Reassess continued GIP need every day.
  9. Plan transition early: Once symptoms can be managed at a lower level, transition to RHC/CHC or another appropriate setting.

Questions that sharpen a GIP conversation

“What symptom is currently uncontrolled?”
“What has already been tried, and what happened?”
“What requires inpatient intensity today?”
“Why can’t this reasonably be managed at home, SNF, ALF, or another lower level right now?”
“If the symptom stabilizes today, what is the discharge/transition plan?”
“Who is reassessing level of care and documenting continued need?”

Your non-clinical field-sales role

  • Recognize the opportunity early and escalate quickly.
  • Get the correct hospice clinician/liaison involved.
  • Clarify who owns the next step.
  • Keep the hospital CM and family informed without making clinical promises.
  • Track bottlenecks: certification, consents, physician calls, contracts, bed/location, discharge plan.
  • Do not tell the CM or family that a patient “qualifies for GIP” before the clinical determination is made.

🤝 Hospice vs Palliative Care

The simplest distinction

Palliative care is an approach to serious illness focused on symptom relief, quality of life, communication, and support. It can be appropriate at any stage of serious illness and may be delivered alongside disease-directed or curative treatment.

Hospice is a defined end-of-life care model and Medicare Part A benefit for terminally ill patients who meet the six-month prognosis requirement and elect hospice.

Full comparison

QuestionPalliative careHospice
Who is it for?People with serious illness who need symptom, communication, psychosocial, or quality-of-life support.People certified as terminally ill with a prognosis of 6 months or less if the illness runs its normal course.
Can disease-directed treatment continue?Yes. Palliative care can coexist with chemotherapy, dialysis, HF treatment, surgery, etc.For the terminal illness and related conditions, the Medicare hospice election shifts to palliative/comfort management rather than Medicare-paid curative treatment outside the hospice arrangement.
Is there a prognosis requirement?Not inherently.Yes—6 months or less if the illness follows its normal course.
Is there a single Medicare “palliative care benefit” like hospice?No universal stand-alone benefit equivalent to hospice. Services may be billed/covered through ordinary Medicare benefits depending on provider, service, and setting.Yes—specific Medicare Part A hospice benefit.
Where can care occur?Hospital, clinic, home, SNF, specialty program, telehealth where allowed, etc.Usually where the patient lives; inpatient respite/GIP available when criteria are met.
Team focusSymptoms, treatment decisions, goals, coping, support, care coordination.All of those plus comprehensive end-of-life services under the hospice benefit and POC.
Family/caregiver supportOften included, program-dependent.Core hospice function; includes psychosocial, spiritual, and bereavement support.

When palliative care may be the better conversation

  • The patient has serious symptoms but still wants aggressive disease-directed therapy.
  • Prognosis is uncertain or clearly longer than six months.
  • The patient/family is not ready to elect hospice but needs goals-of-care help.
  • There is complex decision-making, caregiver stress, or symptom burden earlier in the disease course.

When hospice should move up the list

  • Repeated hospitalizations/ED visits despite maximal treatment.
  • Progressive functional or nutritional decline.
  • The burdens of treatment increasingly outweigh perceived benefit.
  • Patient/family repeatedly says “I don’t want to keep coming back to the hospital.”
  • Goals have clearly shifted from extending life at all costs toward comfort, time at home, and avoiding burdensome interventions.
  • Clinicians believe the prognosis may be 6 months or less if the disease follows its normal course.

Useful talking points

To a family: “Palliative care can support comfort while someone is still pursuing treatment. Hospice is the more comprehensive end-of-life benefit when the illness is advanced enough and the goals have shifted primarily to comfort.”
To a CM: “If they still want disease-directed treatment, palliative may be the better bridge. If the bigger story is repeated decline and they’re saying they don’t want to come back, it may be time for a hospice conversation.”
To a hesitant patient: “A hospice consult is a conversation, not a commitment. You can hear what the service would look like and decide whether it matches your goals.”

Common misconceptions

“Palliative care is just hospice with another name.”

No. Hospice is palliative by nature, but palliative care is broader and can begin much earlier while active treatment continues.

“Hospice means no treatment at all.”

No. Hospice involves active treatment of pain, dyspnea, nausea, anxiety, wounds, infections when appropriate to goals, and many other symptoms. The key shift is away from treatment intended to cure the terminal illness and toward palliation and quality of life.

“Hospice means the patient has exactly six months.”

No. Six months is the physician’s prognosis if the illness runs its normal course. Patients may decline faster, live longer, improve and discharge, or remain eligible through repeated recertifications.

“Hospice means DNR.”

Hospice philosophy and goals typically align with avoiding burdensome resuscitation, but Medicare hospice eligibility itself is not simply a DNR rule. Code-status discussions must remain patient-specific and follow applicable law/policy.

🚨 General Hospice Indicators

Think trajectory, not snapshots

The best referral opportunities often appear when several trends cluster together:

Repeated hospitalizationsED bounce-backsFunctional declineWeight lossPoor intakeMore ADL dependenceRecurrent infectionsFallsIncreasing oxygen needsWorsening cognitionPressure injuriesEscalating symptom burdenTreatment intoleranceCaregiver exhaustion

Five domains to scan mentally

  1. Disease progression: Is the underlying illness clearly advancing?
  2. Function: What could the patient do 1–3 months ago that they cannot do now?
  3. Nutrition: Is intake falling? Weight dropping? Swallowing worsening?
  4. Utilization: Is there a pattern of admissions, ED visits, infections, or rescue treatment?
  5. Goals: Are the patient/family starting to reject burdensome interventions or hospital returns?

Fast field questions

“Who has been back here more than once recently for the same advanced disease?”
“Who has declined the most functionally since their last admission?”
“Any families saying they’re done with the hospital cycle?”
“Who is becoming harder to discharge safely because symptoms or caregiving needs keep escalating?”
“Any patient where the goals have shifted more toward comfort than another procedure or treatment?”

🫀 Disease-Specific Recognition

Use these as prompts—not automatic eligibility rules

Heart disease / CHF
  • Frequent exacerbations, admissions, or ED use.
  • Dyspnea/fatigue at rest or with minimal exertion.
  • Progressive edema or fluid-management difficulty.
  • Declining function despite guideline-directed therapy.
  • Renal dysfunction, hypotension, arrhythmias, or treatment intolerance complicating management.
  • Patient choosing to avoid further hospitalization or invasive therapy.
COPD / advanced pulmonary disease
  • Frequent exacerbations, pneumonia, or admissions.
  • Dyspnea at rest/minimal activity and shrinking functional reserve.
  • Increasing oxygen dependence or support needs.
  • Weight loss/cachexia and fatigue.
  • Hypercapnia/hypoxemia history or recurrent respiratory failure.
  • Patient no longer wanting intubation or repeated acute-care cycles.
Dementia
  • Progressive loss of ambulation, speech, continence, self-feeding, or sitting ability.
  • Dysphagia, aspiration, recurrent pneumonia or UTIs.
  • Weight loss, poor intake, dehydration.
  • Pressure injuries or recurrent skin breakdown.
  • Total-care dependence and increasing frailty.
  • Goals shifting away from hospitalization, feeding tubes, or burdensome interventions.
Renal disease
  • Progressive renal failure with escalating symptom burden.
  • Decision not to start dialysis or to stop dialysis.
  • Fluid overload, pruritus, nausea, encephalopathy, weakness.
  • Repeated admissions or declining tolerance of dialysis.
  • Severe comorbid disease and functional decline.
Liver disease
  • Recurrent ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, infections, or hospitalizations.
  • Progressive jaundice, weakness, poor intake, weight loss.
  • Declining function despite treatment.
  • Not a transplant candidate or no longer pursuing transplant.
Cancer
  • Progressive/metastatic disease despite treatment, or decision to stop disease-directed therapy.
  • Declining performance status.
  • Weight loss/cachexia and poor intake.
  • Increasing pain, dyspnea, nausea, weakness, confusion.
  • Frequent hospital use or complications.
Neurologic disease (ALS, stroke, Parkinsonism, etc.)
  • Progressive weakness and loss of mobility/ADLs.
  • Dysphagia, aspiration, weight loss.
  • Respiratory muscle weakness or recurrent respiratory infection.
  • Communication loss or severe cognitive/behavioral decline depending on disease.
  • Repeated infections or hospitalizations.

About CMS/MAC disease guidelines

CMS national rules center on the six-month prognosis and individualized certification. Disease-specific LCDs are issued by Medicare Administrative Contractors and can differ by jurisdiction. Treat them as documentation frameworks and prognostic guidance, not as rigid “must meet every box” sales scripts.

🧠 FAST Dementia

Late-stage anchor points

7A — speech limited to about six intelligible words.

7B — only one intelligible word.

7C — unable to ambulate independently.

7D — unable to sit up independently.

7E — unable to smile.

7F — unable to hold head up.

Important: FAST stage alone does not determine hospice eligibility. The broader trajectory—including nutrition, infections, aspiration, wounds, function, comorbidities, and overall prognosis—matters.

🏥 Hospital Quick-Shot CM Questions

How to use this in the hospital

You usually have a very short window with a case manager. Don’t run a checklist. Pick one or two questions that fit the moment, listen for the barrier, and then offer one concrete way to help.

Best default opener: “Anything on your list today where the discharge plan is getting hung up and an outside partner might be able to help?”

⚡ Quick CM Question

One fast, general question for the next case manager you approach.

“Anything holding up a discharge today?”
Tap once before moving to the next case manager.

🚪 Discharge-first questions

“What’s the anticipated discharge plan right now?”
“What’s the main barrier keeping this patient here today?”
“What still needs to happen before discharge is realistic?”
“Is the hold-up mainly clinical, functional, caregiver-related, equipment-related, or placement-related?”
“What’s still missing to make the discharge plan workable?”
“Is there anything an outside partner could help solve?”

🏠 Home-readiness questions

“If this patient goes home, what worries you most about the plan holding together?”
“Does the family feel ready to manage them at home?”
“Are there symptoms, equipment needs, medications, or caregiver concerns that could derail discharge?”
“Is the family aligned with the plan, or is there still a difficult conversation that needs to happen?”
“Who is coordinating the next steps from here?”

🔁 Readmission / trajectory questions

“If they go home today, what do you think is most likely to bring them back?”
“Is this one of those patients who keeps coming back with the same problem?”
“What are you seeing with the patient’s overall trajectory compared with a month or two ago?”
“Has their functional status meaningfully declined since the last admission?”
“Are we still fixing a reversible problem, or are we starting to see a bigger pattern of decline?”

❤️ Goals-of-care / hospice-opening questions

“What is the patient or family saying they want at this point?”
“Are they still focused on returning to the hospital for treatment, or are they starting to prioritize comfort and staying home?”
“Has anyone talked with them about hospice yet, or would an informational conversation help?”
“Would it help if our clinical team simply evaluated and explained what support could look like?”
“Do you have anyone where the discharge plan would be easier if the family had stronger support at home?”

🤝 How can I help? — clean offers

“If I can take one thing off your plate on this discharge, what would help most?”
“Would a quick hospice information visit help the family make a decision?”
“Do you want me to get our clinical team involved now so you know whether hospice is a realistic option?”
“If hospice ends up being appropriate, what discharge timing are you working toward?”
“Who should I keep updated so you don’t have to chase us for status?”

Offer only what your team can actually deliver. Avoid promising eligibility, admission timing, equipment, transport, or a level of care before the appropriate team confirms it.

⚡ Three ultra-fast hallway versions

10 seconds
“Anything getting hung up on discharge today where we might be able to help?”
30 seconds
“Anybody whose discharge is difficult because the family isn’t sure they can manage at home—or who keeps bouncing back with the same end-stage problem?”
60 seconds
“Who on your list has the toughest discharge today? What’s the barrier, and if they went home, what’s most likely to bring them right back? If stronger support or a hospice conversation could help, I can get our team involved.”

🎯 Better than “Do you have any hospice patients?”

Broad referral asks put the work on the case manager. These questions are stronger because they start with their problem: getting a safe discharge accomplished, preventing another avoidable return, supporting a worried family, and closing gaps in the home plan.

Your mental sequence: Discharge barrier → home risk → trajectory → goals → offer help → close the loop.

🏢 SNF Probing Questions by Role

How to use these in a nursing facility

Do not interrogate the building or run through a checklist. Pick one or two questions that fit the person’s role, listen for a name or pattern, then ask one follow-up. The strongest questions help the facility solve a problem: avoidable transfers, difficult symptoms, family uncertainty, repeated decline, care-plan mismatch, or residents who are no longer benefiting from a restorative approach.

Simple SNF sequence: What is changing? → What is becoming harder? → What does the resident/family want? → Would a hospice evaluation or informational conversation help?

DON — Director of Nursing

“Who are you most worried about declining right now?”
“Any residents having repeated ER trips or hospitalizations?”
“Who is getting harder for your staff to keep comfortable?”
“Any residents whose symptom burden is increasing despite the current plan?”
“Anybody coming back from the hospital noticeably weaker than before?”
“Any families asking you whether it’s time to focus more on comfort?”
“Who has had the biggest change in function, intake, or cognition lately?”
“Any residents you feel are entering a different phase of their illness?”
“Where could hospice support make life easier for your nursing team?”
“Any residents you want our clinical team to simply take a look at?”

❓ Family & Referral-Source FAQ

Common questions

“Does hospice mean we’re giving up?”

A useful response is: “Hospice changes what we’re fighting for. Instead of trying to cure an illness that is no longer responding, the team concentrates on comfort, dignity, time where the patient wants to be, and support for the family.”

“Does hospice automatically stop every medication?”

No. Medications are reviewed in light of the goals of care, symptom benefit, burdens, and whether they are related to the terminal illness. Many medications continue; others may be simplified or stopped when they no longer help the patient’s goals.

“Do we lose our regular doctor?”

Not necessarily. The patient can designate an attending physician. Hospice coordinates care within Medicare hospice rules.

“Can we call 911?”

A patient always can seek emergency help, but families should understand that uncoordinated hospital/ambulance care related to the terminal illness can create coverage issues and may conflict with the hospice plan. The practical teaching is: call hospice first whenever possible.

“Can hospice be stopped?”

Yes. The patient/representative can revoke hospice at any time in writing and can re-elect later if eligible.

“Can we change hospice companies?”

Yes. Medicare allows a transfer to another hospice once per benefit period; a transfer is not a revocation.

“Does hospice pay for the nursing-home room?”

Usually no. Routine room and board in a nursing facility, assisted living, or residential setting generally is not part of the Medicare hospice benefit. Medicare hospice can cover an arranged inpatient stay when GIP or inpatient respite criteria are met.

“Is morphine what makes someone die faster?”

Hospice uses opioids and other medications to relieve symptoms such as pain and dyspnea, with dosing individualized to the patient. The treatment goal is symptom relief, not hastening death.
